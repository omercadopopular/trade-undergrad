\begin{frame}{Tariffs}
\begin{wideitemize}
    \item Tariffs are import taxes
    \item<2-> Tariffs are wedges (gaps) between the source country and the destination country prices:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \blue{Simple tariffs}: $P = P^* + t$, where $t$ is some dollar amount \
        \qquad (used, e.g., for imports of alcohol and tobacco; more common back in the day)
        \item \blue{Ad valorem tariffs}: $P = P^*(1+\tau)$, where $\tau$ is some percentage \
        \qquad (used for most goods currently)
    \end{itemize}
    \item<3-> Who pays for the tariffs?
    \begin{itemize}
        \item<4-> In an \blue{accounting} sense, importing firms
        \item<5-> In an \blue{economic} sense, it depends on supply and demand elasticity
    \end{itemize}
\end{wideitemize}
    
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Effects of a Tariff in a small country: discussion}
\begin{wideitemize}
    \item In a small country, \blue{domestic import demand does not impact global markets}

    \item<2-> World price $P_W$ not only taken as given, but \blue{unaffected by import demand}

    \item<3-> \textcolor{brown}{Export supply curve (XS)} is infinitely elastic, meaning foreign producers :
    \begin{itemize}
        \item supply \textbf{any} volume of exports at the world price $P_W$, but...
        \item ...supply \textbf{zero} exports at a price below $P_W$
        \item ...supply \textbf{infinite} exports at a price above $P_W$ 
    \end{itemize}
    \item<4-> \blue{Intuition}: flexible production, constant costs, tiny price change $\rightarrow$ massive change in quantity supplied.
    \item<5-> In that case, tariffs are fully passed through to domestic market: $P = P_W + t$
    \item<6-> Reduces imports to $Q_T < Q_W$
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap: Welfare decomposition with a tariff}
  For an importing country that imposes an ad valorem tariff $\tau$:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Domestic price rises: quantity imported falls
    \item Winners: some domestic producers, government (tariff revenue)
    \item Losers: domestic consumers; foreign exporters (lower world price)
  \end{itemize}
  Welfare change = $\Delta CS + \Delta PS + \Delta TR$.
  Typical small-country result: world price unchanged $\Rightarrow$ tariff is pure loss (deadweight loss).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Optimal tariff (large country) --- intuition}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A large country can affect the world price it pays for imports.
    \item By imposing a tariff, the large country improves its terms of trade (ToT): world export price falls.
    \item The ToT gain can, up to a point, offset the domestic efficiency losses (deadweight losses).
    \item There is a tariff level that maximizes domestic welfare: the "optimal tariff".
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Optimal ad valorem tariff: a simple condition}
  Consider a large importer facing an upward-sloping foreign export supply (or downward-sloping world demand). A common approximation:
  \[\tau^{*} \approx \frac{1}{\varepsilon^{x}}\]
  where $\varepsilon^{x}$ is the (absolute value of) elasticity of foreign export supply (or the price responsiveness of world exports). Intuition:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If foreign supply is very elastic ($\varepsilon^{x}\to\infty$) then $\tau^{*}\to 0$.
    \item If foreign supply is inelastic, the importing country can extract large ToT gains.
  \end{itemize}
  (Formal derivations use welfare derivatives and the effect of a tariff on the world price.)
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why "optimal tariff" is a problem for global welfare}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Terms-of-trade manipulation: gains to the imposing country are losses to foreign exporters and their consumers.
    \item Aggregate world welfare typically falls: efficiency losses are not completely redistributed in a way that benefits the world.
    \item If many large countries try to set optimal tariffs simultaneously, the result can be mutually damaging.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tariffs and strategic interaction: the tariff war dilemma}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Two large countries: each would like to improve its own welfare by imposing tariffs (terms-of-trade gains).
    \item If both impose tariffs, retaliatory effects reduce the initial ToT gains and increase efficiency losses.
    \item The interaction resembles a prisoner's dilemma: individually tempting to tariff, collectively worse outcome.
    \item Under retaliation, everybody can lose: ToT gains evaporate, deadweight losses cumulate.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Global losses due to tariffs}
  Key points:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Gains from tariffs for a large country are redistributional: they come from other countries (ToT manipulation).
    \item When others retaliate, the original gains evaporate and deadweight losses accumulate.
    \item Multilateral welfare typically decreases when many countries use tariffs strategically.
  \end{itemize}
  Policy implication: Without cooperative institutions or binding agreements, tariff competition can lead to inefficient global outcomes.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Krugman on "Pop Internationalism" and "Enlightened Mercantilism"}
  Paul Krugman (essay: "Pop Internationalism") critiques simplistic mercantilist views:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Pop internationalism: the popular belief that trade is zero-sum; exports are good, imports are bad.
    \item "Enlightened mercantilism": the idea that governments can reliably use trade policy to improve national welfare (by subsidizing exports, taxing imports).
  \end{itemize}
  Krugman's point: terms-of-trade arguments can look appealing, but political economy, retaliation, and the costs of protection often make mercantilist policies costly in practice.
  (Use this slide to prompt class discussion and contrast theory vs political practice.)
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bastiat: "If goods do not cross borders, soldiers will"}
  Frédéric Bastiat's famous aphorism:
  \begin{quote}
  ``If goods do not cross borders, soldiers will.''
  \end{quote}
  Interpretation:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Economic interdependence reduces incentives for conflict: trade creates mutual stakes in peaceful relations.
    \item Protectionism and autarky can increase geopolitical tensions.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Complex interdependence (Keohane \& Nye)}
  Three defining characteristics:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Multiple channels of interaction (state and non-state actors; economic, social, transnational).
    \item Absence of a hierarchy among issues (no single dominant issue like security).
    \item Declining use of military force as a policy tool among advanced economies.
  \end{enumerate}
  Implication for trade policy:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Economic linkages and institutions matter; cooperation can be sustained through repeated interactions and institutions.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How to avoid the tariff-war dilemma?}
  Countries use two broad strategies:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Multilateral rules and institutions (GATT/WTO) to lock in reciprocal liberalization and provide dispute settlement.
    \item Preferential or regional arrangements (Free Trade Agreements) to lock in liberalization among subsets of countries and provide cooperation structures.
  \end{itemize}
  FTAs can reduce the unilateral temptation to tariff by guaranteeing market access among partners and by creating enforcement/dispute mechanisms.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Free Trade Agreements: motives}
  Principal motives for FTAs:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Secure preferential access to partner markets.
    \item Reduce tariff barriers and regulatory friction between partners.
    \item Deepen integration (investment, services, standards).
    \item Create a political commitment device against protectionism (lock-in).
    \item Geopolitical / strategic considerations.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{US Free Trade Agreements (examples)}
  A non-exhaustive list of notable US FTAs:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item USMCA (United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement; replaced NAFTA)
    \item CAFTA-DR (Central America-Dominican Republic FTA)
    \item KORUS (United States–Korea FTA)
    \item Bilateral FTAs: Chile, Israel, Jordan, Singapore, Australia, Bahrain, Oman, Morocco, Peru, Colombia, Panama
    \item Many of these combine tariff liberalization with rules on services, IP, investment, labor and environment provisions.
    \item Note: FTAs vary widely in depth and scope.
  \end{itemize}
  Note: FTAs vary widely in depth and scope.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Typology of trade agreements}
  Simple typology (increasing depth of integration):
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Preferential Trade Agreement (PTA): tariff preferences for selected partners
    \item Free Trade Area (FTA): remove tariffs among members; members keep own external tariffs (e.g., NAFTA/USMCA)
    \item Customs Union: FTA + common external tariff
    \item Common Market: customs union + free movement of factors (labor, capital)
    \item Economic Union: common market + harmonized economic policies, possibly fiscal transfers
    \item Monetary Union: common currency (e.g., eurozone)
  \end{enumerate}
  Additional dimensions: coverage (goods, services, investment), rules of origin, dispute settlement, regulatory cooperation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The European Union: salient features}
  The EU as a deep integration example:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Began as a customs union and evolved to a single market (four freedoms: goods, services, people, capital).
    \item Common external tariff; supranational institutions (Commission, Parliament, Court of Justice).
    \item Deeper regulatory harmonization and, for many members, a monetary union (euro).
    \item Strong internal dispute and enforcement mechanisms; significant policy coordination.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{GATT $\rightarrow$ WTO: brief history}
  Timeline and milestones:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item 1947: General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) established as provisional multilateral framework after WWII.
    \item Successive negotiation rounds: Kennedy Round (tariff cuts), Tokyo Round (1970s, non-tariff measures), Uruguay Round (1986–1994).
    \item 1995: World Trade Organization (WTO) created as successor to GATT; formalized rules, expanded coverage (services, TRIPS), stronger dispute settlement.
    \item 2001: Doha Round launched — focus on development; progress has been limited.
    \item Recent outcomes: Bali (2013) Trade Facilitation Agreement; Nairobi (2015) agricultural decisions; rise of regional mega-deals (CPTPP, RCEP).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Core WTO principles and tools}
  Core principles:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Most-Favoured-Nation (MFN): equal treatment across trading partners (with some exceptions).
    \item National Treatment: treat foreign and domestic goods equally after entry.
    \item Reciprocity and binding of tariffs through negotiated concessions.
    \item Multilateral dispute settlement: formal process, panel rulings, possible authorized retaliation.
  \end{itemize}
  Purpose: reduce incentives for unilateral ToT-seeking tariffs, provide a forum to resolve conflicts, and stabilize expectations.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why institutions matter}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Institutions reduce uncertainty and the incentive to unilaterally deviate (repeat-game effects).
    \item Dispute settlement reduces destructive retaliation cycles by providing legal/binding remedies.
    \item Multilateral rule-making makes cooperation among many players feasible (solves coordination problems).
  \end{itemize}
  However: political constraints, distributional conflicts, and rising protectionist pressures pose ongoing challenges.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Concluding remarks}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Optimal tariffs highlight a strategic motive for protection, but ToT gains are often offset by retaliation and global efficiency losses.
    \item FTAs and the WTO are institutional responses to the tariff-war dilemma: they lock in reciprocal liberalization and provide enforcement mechanisms.
    \item Political economy and history matter: the pattern of agreements (bilateral, regional, multilateral) reflects strategic, economic, and political trade-offs.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
